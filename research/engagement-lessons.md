# Westward research: why The Oregon Trail engaged classrooms, and what transfers

Research pass on the engagement design of MECC's *The Oregon Trail* (1971 onward, especially the 1985 Apple II version and its 1990 MS-DOS port) and on what Westward can borrow. Checked October 2026.

Confidence labels (same scheme as `california-and-design.md`):
- **verified**: confirmed in a source I read directly (designer's own writing, research paper, institutional page).
- **partly verified**: supported, but only by a secondary source, a summary, or a single report.
- **uncertain**: plausible or widely repeated, but I could not confirm it in a source I could read. Do not put these in student-facing text.

Westward context assumed throughout: 40-minute lesson, 4 students per Chromebook (Navigator, Quartermaster, Journal keeper, Family doctor), four groups playing four families, 1849 to 1854, event cards, procedural trail events, low-poly diorama, soft timer, and a "Who Decided?" ending ledger.

## 1. Design history and intent

### The 1971 original (Rawitsch, Heinemann, Dillenberger)

- Don Rawitsch, a Carleton College senior student-teaching 8th-grade US history, wanted "a way to get his students to participate in history." He started a "rudimentary board game"; his roommates and fellow student teachers Bill Heinemann and Paul Dillenberger suggested turning it into a computer game. **verified**. [MNopedia, "Oregon Trail (computer game)"](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/oregon-trail-computer-game)
- It debuted in Rawitsch's class on December 3, 1971. The school had one teletype and no other computing hardware, so "students played the game in groups of five; with only one machine, the groups had to take turns." **verified**. [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/oregon-trail-computer-game)
  - This matters for Westward: the original classroom Oregon Trail was a shared, group game around one terminal, not a solo game. Alan Gershenfeld (EdSurge) makes the same point: limited computer access led to group play that sparked "debate and discussion around the players' choices." **partly verified** (Gershenfeld's account, not a primary source). [EdSurge, Gershenfeld, Oct 11, 2022](https://edsurge.com/news/2022-10-11-reflections-on-50-years-of-game-based-learning-part-1)
- Dillenberger on the intent: "It's trying to have the student experience what it was like without having them in danger themselves." **verified** (as quoted). [FOX 13, "Oregon Trail 50 years later"](https://www.fox13news.com/news/oregon-trail-50-years-later-how-3-minnesota-student-teachers-blazed-a-new-trail-in-gaming)
- The game was originally on a UNIVAC timeshare reached by teletype; Rawitsch deleted it at semester's end and kept a printout. MECC hired him in 1974, and he "read diaries of Oregon Trail pioneers" and tied events to places (for example, mountain events only after about 950 miles). **verified**. [Smithsonian Magazine, Matt Jancer, July 22, 2016](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/forgotten-history-oregon-trail-how-it-was-made-180959851/)
- He "modified the frequency and details of the random events... to more accurately reflect the accounts he had read in the historical diaries." **partly verified** (Wikipedia summary of sources). [Wikipedia, The Oregon Trail (series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oregon_Trail_(series))
  - Lesson: the randomness was not arbitrary. Probabilities were tuned to primary sources. Westward's procedural events should be too.
- On MECC's statewide timeshare, OREGON "remained the most popular program on the system" until the system shut down in 1983. **verified** (Bouchard's history). [died-of-dysentery.com, "The Earliest Versions"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/early-versions.html)
- Original hunting required typing a word quickly. **uncertain** (widely repeated as typing "BANG"; I did not confirm the exact word in a source I could read).

### The 1985 redesign (R. Philip Bouchard and the MECC team)

Bouchard led the 1985 Apple II design and has written extensively about it on his site and in *You Have Died of Dysentery: The Creation of Oregon Trail* ([book listing](https://www.thevideogamelibrary.org/book/you-have-died-of-dysentery-the-creation-of-oregon-trail)). I read his site essays, not the book.

- **Goal: both fun and educational.** Bouchard aimed for a product "highly entertaining, in addition to being clearly educational," believing he could "do both, without seriously compromising either, but had to strike a careful balance." **verified**. [Bouchard, "Imagining the New 1985 Design"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/imagining-appleII.html)
- **Point-to-point journey.** The trip became "around 16 segments, of varying but similar lengths, each ending in a landmark." **verified**. [Bouchard, "Imagining"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/imagining-appleII.html)
- **Talking to people** at landmarks was added as "an important method for obtaining helpful hints and discovering historical and geographic details." **verified**. [Bouchard, "Imagining"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/imagining-appleII.html)
- **Naming party members** was added for investment: the player provides "four additional names as traveling companions. If you make bad decisions or encounter a streak of bad luck, then members of your family will die." **verified**. [Bouchard, "Imagining"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/imagining-appleII.html)
- **Why tombstones:** "This humorous interlude compensates for the player's failure to complete the journey." The tombstone data was saved so later players could see it. **verified** (the quote); **partly verified** (exact persistence behavior on school disks). [Bouchard in Format Magazine, July 24, 2017](https://www.format.com/magazine/you-have-died-of-dysentery-oregon-trail-design/); [Bouchard, "Imagining"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/imagining-appleII.html)
- **Why the specific diseases:** Bouchard "incorporated five diseases or symptoms often named by travelers on the Oregon Trail: typhoid, cholera, measles, dysentery, exhaustion, and fever. Oddly, one of these five has captured the popular imagination." The death messages are specific because diarists named these specific causes. **verified**. [Format Magazine](https://www.format.com/magazine/you-have-died-of-dysentery-oregon-trail-design/)
- The 1985 team removed the earlier "medicine and doctor system" as historically inaccurate and replaced it with named diseases. **partly verified** (Wikipedia). [Wikipedia, The Oregon Trail (1985)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oregon_Trail_(1985_video_game))
  - Relevant to Westward's **Family doctor** role: frame it as a caregiver with period remedies of limited effect (rest, clean water, slower pace), not as a role that can cure.
- **Why hunting:** Hunting was "by far the best known part of the game, and the main reason that many kids... enjoyed playing." The team wanted "a bona fide skills-based arcade-style game" set in the actual terrain. **verified**. [Bouchard, "The Hunting Activity"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/hunting.html); [Format Magazine](https://www.format.com/magazine/you-have-died-of-dysentery-oregon-trail-design/)
- **Why the 100-pound carry limit:** realism ("a practical limit to how much meat the hunter could actually carry back") and "I also wanted the kids to think about the concept of wastefulness, without explicitly mentioning it." **verified**. [Bouchard, "The Hunting Activity"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/hunting.html)
- **Difficulty and replay:** "To encourage replay, I built three difficulty levels... banker, carpenter, and farmer," tied to the point multiplier. He resisted pressure to make hunting easier because testing showed kids "developed a keen proficiency quite quickly." **verified**. [Format Magazine](https://www.format.com/magazine/you-have-died-of-dysentery-oregon-trail-design/); [Bouchard, "The Hunting Activity"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/hunting.html)
- The team deliberately made the game hard enough that most first-time players fail but want to try again ("What makes you want to go back over and over again?"). **partly verified** (UGA profile paraphrase plus quote). [UGA Honors, Bouchard profile](https://honors.uga.edu/r-philip-bouchard-oregon-trailblazer-creating-the-classic-80s-version-of-the-oregon-trail/)
- **Playtesting:** "It is also crucial to investigate how real users react to the product... as soon in the development cycle as possible." **verified**. [Format Magazine](https://www.format.com/magazine/you-have-died-of-dysentery-oregon-trail-design/) Two concrete results from testing:
  - The alpha travel screen was boring; a tiny animated wagon fixed it: "Instead of being bored with this screen, kids paid very close attention to it." Travel ran automatically, "only stopping if an important event occurred." **verified**. [Bouchard, "The Travel Screen"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/travel-screen.html)
  - Hiring a guide at rivers originally cut risk by 50%; testers concluded "hiring a guide was useless," so it was raised to 80%. Rivers were capped at four because "after 3 or 4 crossings, there would be little educational value in including more." **verified**. [Bouchard, "Crossing Rivers"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/crossing-rivers.html)
- River outcomes came from a model of depth, width, current, bottom type, and recent rain. **verified**. [Bouchard, "Crossing Rivers"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/crossing-rivers.html)
- Score combined ending condition, supplies, and profession, shown on a high-score table (the "Top Ten"). **partly verified** (Wikipedia). [Wikipedia, The Oregon Trail (1985)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oregon_Trail_(1985_video_game))
- The 1985 version was first aimed at the home market; schools got it through MECC site licenses from 1985 to 1986. The 1990 DOS version was a port with redrawn graphics, not a redesign. **verified** (Bouchard's history). [Bouchard, "A Brief History"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/brief-history.html)

## 2. Evidence about classroom engagement

- **Immediate pull in 1971.** "Students came to class at seven o'clock in the morning and stayed until the last teacher left the building just to play the game." **verified** (as reported). [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/oregon-trail-computer-game) Heinemann: "there were kids in line from early in the morning... to well after school," and "The next kid would sit down and say yes [to play again], and it would just be active all day long." **verified** (as quoted). [FOX 13](https://www.fox13news.com/news/oregon-trail-50-years-later-how-3-minnesota-student-teachers-blazed-a-new-trail-in-gaming)
- **Scale.** Series sales of over 65 million by 2011; by 1994 it was about a third of MECC's roughly $30 million revenue. **partly verified**. [Wikipedia, 1985 game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oregon_Trail_(1985_video_game)) Bouchard: "the greatest penetration in North American schools of all educational software products." **verified** (his claim). [Bouchard, "A Brief History"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/brief-history.html)
- **"Oregon Trail Generation."** Anna Garvey coined the term in a 2015 Social Media Week piece for people who grew up between analog and digital life (roughly born 1977 to 1983). **partly verified** (Wikipedia summary; original article not read). [Wikipedia, Xennials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xennials)
- **What players remember most** is the memes, which Bouchard catalogs: dysentery ("The lesson that first-time players... always learn") and tombstones are among the eight he lists. He notes "you have to know the game in order to understand the humor." **verified**. [Bouchard, "Memes"](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/memes.html) In other words, the lasting memories are about **loss of named people, failure, and shared jokes**, not facts about forts. That is both the opportunity and the warning.
- **It still works on today's kids.** A third-grade teacher in California played the old game with Gen Alpha students; they yelled at deaths and disasters ("Bennett, you had a snakebite!"), moving "from quiet typing to pure dismay." **partly verified** (popular press anecdote). [Upworthy](https://www.upworthy.com/millennial-teacher-shares-oregon-trail-with-her-gen-alpha-students-and-the-trauma-is-real/)
- **Why it worked, per a learning-games designer:** "the organic alignment of core game mechanics and learning objectives," plus it respected kids with "scary and provocative, and truly memorable" content. **partly verified** (expert opinion). [EdSurge, Gershenfeld](https://edsurge.com/news/2022-10-11-reflections-on-50-years-of-game-based-learning-part-1)
- **Caution on learning claims.** I found no rigorous study showing the classic Oregon Trail improved history learning. Its evidence is popularity and memory, not measured outcomes. **verified as an absence** in this search pass; treat any "studies show Oregon Trail taught history" claim as **uncertain**.
- **General theory that fits:** games pull players by satisfying needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Ryan, Rigby, Przybylski, *Motivation and Emotion* 30(4), 2006). Oregon Trail's choices (autonomy), learnable hunting and river decisions (competence), and named companions plus group play (relatedness) line up with this. **verified** (the study); the mapping to Oregon Trail is my inference. [Rochester PDF](https://www.rochester.edu/warner/lida/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/02bfe513dd59366750000000.pdf); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/12/061226134706.htm)

## 3. Engagement mechanics and the psychology behind them

| Mechanic | What Oregon Trail did | Why it engages | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naming party members | Player supplies four companion names (often friends) | Personal investment; deaths become stories about "us" | [Bouchard](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/imagining-appleII.html) **verified** |
| Emergent stories | Random events plus named people produce unique runs | Each group has a different, retellable story ("Bennett got a snakebite") | [Upworthy anecdote](https://www.upworthy.com/millennial-teacher-shares-oregon-trail-with-her-gen-alpha-students-and-the-trauma-is-real/) **partly verified** |
| Permadeath and loss | Named members die from period diseases; game ends when all die | Stakes; emotional memory. Specific causes come from diaries | [Format](https://www.format.com/magazine/you-have-died-of-dysentery-oregon-trail-design/) **verified** |
| Scarcity and tradeoffs | Food, oxen, parts, money; pace and rations; ford, float, or ferry | Meaningful choices with visible costs; luck plus skill | [Crossing Rivers](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/crossing-rivers.html) **verified** |
| Randomness and "one more try" | Hard enough to fail; three difficulty levels to encourage replay | Failure feels like bad luck plus fixable mistakes, so players retry | [Format](https://www.format.com/magazine/you-have-died-of-dysentery-oregon-trail-design/); [UGA](https://honors.uga.edu/r-philip-bouchard-oregon-trailblazer-creating-the-classic-80s-version-of-the-oregon-trail/) **verified / partly verified** |
| Top Ten list | High-score table with profession multiplier | Social comparison within a class; a reason to retry | [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oregon_Trail_(1985_video_game)) **partly verified** |
| Hunting as change of pace | Short arcade skill game between text-heavy stretches | Competence, active control, break from reading | [Hunting](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/hunting.html) **verified** |
| Shared play at one machine | 1971 classes played in groups of five | Debate over choices; spectators stay invested in named people | [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/oregon-trail-computer-game) **verified** |
| Humor | Tombstone epitaphs; terse death lines | "Compensates for the player's failure"; makes loss bearable and shareable | [Format](https://www.format.com/magazine/you-have-died-of-dysentery-oregon-trail-design/) **verified** |
| Tombstones for the next player | Saved tombstones visible to later players on the same disk | Leaves a trace; a cross-class conversation | [Bouchard](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/imagining-appleII.html) **partly verified** (details of persistence) |
| Landmarks and pacing | ~16 segments; auto-travel that stops only for events; animated wagon | Steady rhythm of watch, decide, watch; no dead time | [Travel Screen](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/travel-screen.html) **verified** |
| Talking to people | NPC conversations at landmarks give hints and history | History is delivered as useful information, not a lecture | [Bouchard](https://died-of-dysentery.com/stories/imagining-appleII.html) **verified** |

Notes:
- The hunting "waste" lesson was intentionally implicit ("without explicitly mentioning it"). Implicit lessons are easy to miss; Westward should pair any such mechanic with a debrief question. **verified** (the intent); the risk of missing it is my inference.
- The humor worked partly because the game was otherwise serious. Humor about death in a game that now includes Native peoples, Californios, and Chinese miners needs different handling (see section 6).

## 4. Group play around one computer

- **One mouse leaves the others out.** In a study of 60 elementary children with Single Display Groupware, the partner without the mouse tried to touch the screen about 8 times per 10 minutes, looked away about 3 times, and voiced frustration about once. With two input devices, partners "smiled more, and looked at each other more often," peer teaching rose, and "the passive partner desires to maintain some level of control or involvement." **verified**. [Stewart, Raybourn, Bederson, Druin, CHI 98, "When Two Hands Are Better Than One"](https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/hcil/pad++/papers/chi-98-sdg/index.html); [Stewart, Bederson, Druin 1999, "Single Display Groupware"](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/986)
- **How control passes matters.** Inkpen, Booth, Gribble, and Klawe (CHI 95, "Give and Take") studied 132 children aged 9 to 13 sharing one computer. Girls did better when the active player had to *give* control; boys did better when either could *take* it. There were "fewer struggles over control when using two mice," and in Give mode active players re-engaged partners with "Here, you try now." **verified**. [CHI 95 short paper](https://chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/shortppr/ki_bdy.htm)
- An earlier UBC report found that the mix of gender and number of computers shaped outcomes, with female pairs on two separate machines doing worst. **partly verified** (summary only). [UBC TR-94-05](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/tr/1994/tr-94-05)
- Scott, Mandryk, and Inkpen (2003) review children's co-located collaboration around shared displays. **partly verified** (citation only; not read). *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 19(2), 220 to 228.
- **Co-located console play** works as a "computational meeting place," with people taking distinct roles (player, coach, spectator) in group sessions. **partly verified** (abstract). [Voida and Greenberg, "Wii All Play"](https://ucalgary.scholaris.ca/items/a4ab48d2-9860-4200-9256-f692a6819ad3/full)
- **Cooperative learning research (Johnson and Johnson)** names the conditions that stop one student from doing all the work: positive interdependence (including **role** and **resource** interdependence, where each member holds something the group needs), individual accountability, face-to-face promotive interaction, social skills, and group processing. **partly verified** (secondary summaries). [Wikipedia, Cooperative learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_learning); [UNI InTime summary](https://intime.uni.edu/chap-4-positive-interdependence-and-individual-accountability)
- **Roles that commit to a position engage more.** In Reacting to the Past, students in roles aligned to a faction with a committed view gained more self-efficacy than "indeterminate" roles; Reacting students overall reported more engagement and perceived learning than comparison classes. **verified**. [Bledsoe and Richardson 2022, IJTLHE 33(3)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1366192.pdf)

What this means for one Chromebook and four roles (my synthesis from the above):
1. **Split information, not just labels.** Each role should see or hold something the others need (Navigator: map, weather, river depth; Quartermaster: inventory and money; Doctor: each person's health and symptoms; Journal keeper: diary excerpts and the group's record). Resource interdependence beats a cosmetic title.
2. **Make the keyboard pass on a rule, using "give."** Rotate the driver at each landmark, with the outgoing driver handing it over. This follows the Give protocol, which reduced control struggles and worked better for girls.
3. **Require a short role sign-off on big decisions.** For example, a river crossing needs a "ready" tap from Navigator and Quartermaster; a pace change needs the Doctor. This forces talk without slowing every screen.
4. **Give non-drivers a job during travel.** The Journal keeper writes one line per landmark; the Doctor watches health; the Navigator calls the next landmark. Spectators who are watching for something stay engaged, as with the animated wagon.
5. **Debrief group process**, not only history (Johnson and Johnson's "group processing"): one ledger question on how the family decided.

## 5. Critiques, and how later games responded

### Critiques of Oregon Trail

- **Bill Bigelow (Rethinking Schools, 1997)** on the CD-ROM *Oregon Trail II*: it is "sexist, racist, culturally insensitive, and contemptuous of the earth," but teachers can use it to build critical computer literacy. **partly verified** (I could read only the preview). [Rethinking Schools, "On the Road to Cultural Bias"](https://rethinkingschools.org/articles/on-the-road-to-cultural-bias/)
- **ICT News (2017):** Bigelow, "There's nothing in the game that alerts you to the impact your choices are having on indigenous people." Historian Katrine Barber: "Part of why the game is dangerous is because you can pretend you're learning history." Bobbie Conner (Tamástslikt Cultural Institute): players learn "the settling of the West, but not the way the U.S. government steamrolled lots of people." **verified** (as quoted in ICT). [ICT News, "It's a White Thing"](https://ictnews.org/archive/white-thing-oregon-trail-game-doesnt-tell-complete-history/)
- **Rawitsch himself** (Reddit AMA, Feb 1, 2016): "It would have been interesting to add a Native American viewpoint, perhaps a character who watches the wagons come into that territory." He also said games let people "'test' the results of alternative historical outcomes." **verified** (as reported). [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/amp/arts/the-oregon-trail-could-have-used-a-native-american-viewpoint-says-co-creator) At a GDC panel he said that today he "would create the Native American version." **partly verified** (search summary of ICT). [ICT News](https://ictnews.org/archive/white-thing-oregon-trail-game-doesnt-tell-complete-history/)
- **David G. Lewis** (Grand Ronde ethnohistorian): "every inch of the trail is claimed by one or more tribes"; "The tribal story must be a significant and equal party of that story." **verified**. [NDN History Research, Dec 14, 2023](https://ndnhistoryresearch.com/2023/12/14/integrating-tribal-perspectives-into-an-oregon-trail-history/)

### The 2021 Gameloft remake (Apple Arcade)

- Creative director Jarrad Trudgen brought in **three Indigenous historians**. Documented names:
  - **Margaret Huettl** (University of Nebraska-Lincoln, Lac Courte Oreilles descent). **verified**. [KQED MindShift / NPR](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/57853/an-updated-oregon-trail-gives-native-americans-better-representation); [UNL News](https://news.unl.edu/newsrooms/today/article/huettls-oregon-trail-work-featured-in-science-caf-series)
  - **David Lewis** (Oregon State University, Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde). **partly verified**: Northwest News Network lists him among the historians, but KQED's version quotes him only as commentary. [NW News Network](https://www.nwnewsnetwork.org/history-and-culture/2021-05-11/remaking-oregon-trail-video-game-with-an-eye-toward-more-accurate-native-american-depictions)
  - Third historian: **not named** in any source I found. **uncertain**.
- Changes they pushed for: no "broken stilted English" (Native characters speak fluent English, since many were multilingual); "back off the drums and flutes"; varied hair and tribe-specific clothing instead of braids on everyone; rifles instead of bows and arrows for trappers; new roles "not just roles as guides or trappers"; better names; playable Native characters. Opening message: "For Indigenous Peoples, westward expansion was not an adventure but an invasion." Huettl: "They listened to what we were saying." **verified**. [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/57853/an-updated-oregon-trail-gives-native-americans-better-representation); [NW News Network](https://www.nwnewsnetwork.org/history-and-culture/2021-05-11/remaking-oregon-trail-video-game-with-an-eye-toward-more-accurate-native-american-depictions); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/new-oregon-trail-game-features-playable-native-american-characters-180977744/)

### Mission US (WNET, with ASHP/CUNY; evaluated by EDC)

- **Design practice:** history content from the American Social History Project; EDC focus groups test "designs, scripts and game approaches with students and teachers"; direct consultation with portrayed communities. **verified**. [Mission US, Process and Development](https://www.mission-us.org/about/creating-mission-us/process-and-development/)
- **A Cheyenne Odyssey** was built with representatives of the Northern Cheyenne Tribe at Chief Dull Knife College, who consulted on content, scripting, design, and casting; advisors included Richard Littlebear (president, Chief Dull Knife College), Christina Gish Hill, Ann Fabian, and Jeffrey Ostler. **verified**. [Credits: A Cheyenne Odyssey](https://www.mission-us.org/about/our-team/credits/credits-a-cheyenne-odyssey/)
- **Ethical design principles:** safety first (content advisories), accessibility (games split into parts playable in 10 to 20 minutes), evidence-based, respect for people portrayed, continuous improvement. **partly verified** (search summary of the page). [Mission US About](https://mission-us.org/about/)
- **Research findings:**
  - 2011 EDC comparison study, 1,118 middle schoolers: Mission US students outperformed peers on history knowledge and document analysis. 2016 *Up from the Dust* quasi-experiment: 15% knowledge gain vs under 1%. 2020 TimeSnap VR: greater gains in knowledge, causation, perspective, and document analysis. **verified** (Mission US's own summary of EDC reports). [Mission US, Research and Outcomes](https://mission-us.org/about/proven-impact/research-and-outcomes/)
  - **2025 IES-funded randomized trial** (EDC; 60 schools, 25 states, 1,305 students in grades 8 to 10): **no overall significant difference** from business-as-usual. Students in well-resourced schools did better with Mission US; students in under-resourced schools did better with business-as-usual. Teachers in Mission US classrooms reported higher student engagement. **verified**. [Mission US IES research summary (PDF)](https://assets.mission-us.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/28182538/Mission-US-508c-IES-research-summary-5.28.25.pdf)
  - Takeaway: engagement is real, but learning gains depend on the classroom around the game (discussion, documents, decision trackers). The game alone is not the lesson.
- **Flight to Freedom controversy (2017):** a Phoenix district paused the slavery game after parents and activists objected; ASU professor Neal Lester: "it's a horrible idea to move slavery into the realm of gaming." WNET answered that scripts were reviewed by historians and backed by 300+ pages of teacher materials. **verified** (as reported). [KJZZ](https://www.kjzz.org/2017-10-12/content-550387-asu-professor-discusses-criticism-over-slavery-computer-game); [Current, WNET letter](https://current.org/2017/11/letter-to-the-editor-great-care-went-into-design-of-flight-to-freedom/)
  - Takeaway for Westward: families and communities should know in advance what students will encounter, and the hardest history (violence against Native peoples and Chinese miners) belongs in the ledger and debrief, not as something students play through for points.

### Reacting to the Past

- Elaborate historical role-play games (Mark Carnes, Barnard, 1990s), used at 350+ colleges. Students reported more engagement, perceived learning, and self-efficacy; committed-faction roles gained most. **verified**. [Bledsoe and Richardson 2022](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1366192.pdf) Carnes argues the games channel competitiveness into learning. **partly verified** (publisher summary). [Minds on Fire, book listing](https://wordupbooks.com/book/9780674984097)
- Takeaway: give each Westward role a clear stake and point of view, and let the four families argue from their positions in the debrief.

### Brenda Romero, "The Mechanic is the Message"

- A six-game series on historical trauma (including *The New World* on the slave trade, *Síochán Leat* on Cromwell in Ireland, *One Falls for Each of Us* on the Trail of Tears, and *Train*). **verified**. [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/brenda-romero-train-board-game-holocaust); [The Strong Museum of Play](https://www.museumofplay.org/blog/brenda-romero-the-mechanic-is-the-message/)
- In *Train*, players efficiently load yellow pegs into boxcars from typewritten rules, then a card reveals the destination is Auschwitz. The intended emotion is complicity: "People blindly follow rules." **verified**. [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/brenda-romero-train-board-game-holocaust)
- Takeaway: meaning comes from what the rules make you do. Oregon Trail's rules reward arriving with supplies and say nothing about whose land you crossed; that silence is itself a message (Bigelow's point). Westward's "Who Decided?" ledger is a Romero-style reveal: the family plays its own survival game, then sees who else bore the cost. Romero's games are for adults and reflective settings; for 9th graders, the reveal needs a guided debrief, not shock.

## 6. Recommendations for Westward

Ranked by how much engagement each adds per minute of a 40-minute lesson, and how safely it fits history-accuracy goals. Evidence for each is in the sections above; adaptations are my design proposals.

### Top 10 transferable mechanics

1. **Interdependent roles with split information** (sections 4, 5). Each role holds data or a power the group needs; big decisions need a quick sign-off from the relevant roles. *Adaptation:* the Doctor alone sees symptoms, the Quartermaster alone sees stock and cash, the Navigator alone sees river depth and weather, the Journal keeper alone sees the diary quote that hints at the right call. This is the single biggest fix for "one kid at the keyboard."
2. **Students name the people in their family** (Bouchard, section 1). *Adaptation:* the historical family head stays fixed, but students name the children or companions, chosen from a short list of period names (from census or diary sources) or typed freely with a teacher toggle. Suggest *not* using classmates' names, since deaths will be read aloud and shared.
3. **Hard tradeoffs at a few big decision points** (river model, guide tuning). Keep about 3 or 4 major choices (Bouchard capped rivers at four for the same reason). *Adaptation:* each choice shows period options (ford, caulk and float, ferry, wait; Cape Horn vs Panama vs overland), risks shaped by real conditions, and a cost the Quartermaster must pay. Playtest so that "safe" options are actually worth it (the guide lesson).
4. **Landmark rhythm with auto-travel that stops only for events** (Travel Screen). *Adaptation:* the diorama wagon moves on its own between stops, pausing for cards. Non-drivers each watch one indicator. Use the soft timer to shorten travel segments for groups that fall behind, never to cut decisions.
5. **Source-tuned randomness and named, specific causes** (Rawitsch diaries; Bouchard diseases). *Adaptation:* every procedural event and death cause links to a diary or record in `research/`, and the Journal keeper can tap "where is this from?" to see it. Randomness explains why families differ, which sets up the ledger.
6. **Loss with dignity: a class memorial wall instead of joke tombstones** (Bouchard's compensation idea). *Adaptation:* when a family member dies, the group writes a one-line remembrance in a period voice ("Buried near the Platte; we marked it with wagon boards"). Show the wall across groups and periods on that device. Keep the "compensation for failure" function, drop the mockery. Include memorial lines for non-player people named in the ledger.
7. **The four families as the "one more try"** (replay loop). There is no time to replay in 40 minutes, so the comparison across four groups *is* the replay: same trail, different starting positions and luck. *Adaptation:* the ledger shows each family's outcome next to what the same years meant for Native peoples, Californios, and Chinese miners, and asks which outcomes were choice, luck, or law.
8. **A meaningful class leaderboard** (Top Ten). *Adaptation:* rank on things worth rewarding: "people who arrived," "journal entries with evidence," "decisions where every role signed off," or "ledger answers backed by a source." Never rank by money, gold, or land claimed.
9. **One brief, non-violent skill moment** (hunting as change of pace, with an implied waste lesson). *Adaptation:* a 30 to 60 second task such as steering a ford across a current, choosing a safe line through a mountain pass, rationing water in the desert stretch, or panning that shows diminishing returns. Pass the device to a different role for it. No shooting at animals in herds, and never at people.
10. **Talking to people at landmarks, rebuilt as encounters with equals** (Bouchard's NPCs; Gameloft fixes). *Adaptation:* Native, Californio, and Chinese characters speak fluent language, have their own goals, and can refuse or set terms (tolls, trade, ferry rights, which were historically common). Get review from people connected to the communities shown, as Gameloft and Mission US did. Brief humor can live in emigrant diary voices, never at the expense of the people being displaced.

### Five things NOT to copy

1. **Hunting as shooting, with the herd as unlimited ammo targets.** It was the most loved part of Oregon Trail, but in a game about 1849 to 1854 it reads as glorified overhunting, and the jump to violence is short. Use a non-violent skill moment (item 9). (Bouchard's own waste lesson was implicit and easy to miss.)
2. **Native peoples as background helpers or threats, broken English, drums and flutes, uniform braids.** These were exactly what Gameloft's Indigenous historians removed. Also avoid making any group a random "event card" hazard.
3. **Joke tombstones that persist for the next class.** Epitaphs typed by 9th graders, possibly with classmates' names, will produce cruelty. Use the curated memorial wall (item 6) and teacher moderation.
4. **Wealth or supplies as the score.** Ranking by what a family accumulates rewards the same land and gold rush the ledger asks students to question (Bigelow's critique; Romero's point that rules carry meaning).
5. **Letting the game stand in for the lesson, or letting students play the worst events.** The 2025 Mission US trial showed engagement without guaranteed learning gains, and *Flight to Freedom* showed the backlash from simulating atrocity. Keep massacres and expulsions in the ledger and debrief with sources, tell families what the unit covers, and protect the final minutes for discussion.
