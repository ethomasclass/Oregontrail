# Westward research: California, the Chinese route, and game design

Research pass for the California half of Westward (1849 to 1854) and for design pitfalls.
Checked October 2026. Companion data file: `research/california-sources.json` (ids match the game's `sources` fields).

Confidence labels:
- **verified**: confirmed in an authoritative source I read directly (archive, museum, university, government, court record, or the primary text itself).
- **partly verified**: supported, but only by a secondary source, by sources that disagree on a detail, or by a source I could only see in summary.
- **disputed**: historians or sources disagree, or the common version is wrong.

## PART A: California and the Chinese route

### 1. Gold discovery and the rush

- **January 24, 1848**: James Marshall found gold flakes in the tailrace of Sutter's sawmill at Coloma on the South Fork of the American River. The date was fixed by Henry Bigler's diary. **verified**. [California State Parks, Marshall Gold Discovery SHP brochure](https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/484/files/MarshallGoldWebBrochure2013.pdf); [California Historical Landmark 530, Gold Discovery Site](https://ohp.parks.ca.gov/ListedResources/Detail/530)
- Coloma was Nisenan and foothill Miwok land. State Parks: "For thousands of years, the Nisenan and foothill Miwok people built their dome-shaped houses... They called their home along the American River 'Cullumah,' now known as Coloma." **verified**. [State Parks brochure](https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/484/files/MarshallGoldWebBrochure2013.pdf)
- **About 300,000 people** came to California between 1848 and about 1855. This is the standard estimate (Smithsonian NMAH "Many Voices" exhibit: "The influx of some 300,000 people"). **partly verified** (widely cited estimate; I could see the Smithsonian page only in a search summary). Non-Native population was "almost 100,000" by the end of 1849 (PBS American Experience). [Smithsonian NMAH](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/many-voices/online/peopling-expanding-nation-1776%E2%80%931900/incorporating-western-lands/gold-rush); [PBS American Experience, "California"](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/goldrush-california/)
- Madley (USC lecture summary): 13,000 to 14,000 non-Indians lived in California before the gold strike; "By 1860, there were over 360,000 newcomers." **verified** (as Madley's figures). [USC Shoah Foundation Center for Advanced Genocide Research](https://dornsife.usc.edu/cagr/2016/10/31/benjamin-madley-lecture-summary/)

### 2. Who got rich: miners vs. merchants

- Economic historians Karen Clay and Randall Jones (Journal of Economic History, 2008), using the 1850 and 1852 censuses: "economic outcomes were generally small or even zero for miners but were positive and large for nonminers." **verified**. [Cambridge Core abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S002205070800079X/type/journal_article)
- Robert Margo (NBER, 1997): real wages "rose sharply during the early years of the Rush (1848-1852)" and then declined. **verified**. [NBER h0101](https://www.nber.org/papers/h0101)
- Average placer earnings fell from about **$20 a day in 1848 to about $6 a day by 1852 to 1853**. **partly verified**: figures trace to Rodman Paul, *California Gold* (Harvard, 1947); I found them only in secondary summaries, not in Paul directly. Use "about" and do not show a year-by-year table.
- Gold output: "$10 million in 1849, $41 million... in 1850, $75 million in 1851, and $81 million in 1852." **verified**. [PBS American Experience](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/goldrush-california/)
- Shirley Letters (Indian Bar, April 10, 1852): "Gold-mining is nature's great lottery scheme. A man may work in a claim for many months, and be poorer at the end of the time than when he commenced, or he may take out thousands in a few hours. It is a mere matter of chance." **verified** (public domain text). See section 8.
- Prices: 1849 reports of eggs and slices of bread at a dollar each; work boots that cost $3 to $15 in the East selling for $30 to $40. **partly verified** (secondary: PBS Food and Smithsonian Magazine, which draw on Bayard Taylor and E. G. Buffum's *Six Months in the Gold Mines*, 1850). Prices swung wildly by place and month; avoid a single exact multiplier. [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/gold-rush-california-was-much-more-expensive-todays-dot-com-boom-california-180956788/); [PBS Food](https://www.pbs.org/food/the-history-kitchen/food-california-gold-rush/)
- **Sam Brannan**: shouted "Gold! Gold! Gold! Gold from the American River!" in San Francisco (May 1848); "His store made enormous profits by selling as much as $5,000... in goods per day to miners"; "During the 1850s and 1860s Brannan was known as the richest man in California." **verified**. [PBS American Experience, Samuel Brannan](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/goldrush-samuel-brannan)
- **Levi Strauss**: "journeyed to San Francisco in 1853 to make his fortune, though he wouldn't make it panning gold." He sold dry goods wholesale. The riveted-jeans patent came much later: "granted to Jacob Davis and Levi Strauss & Company on May 20, 1873." **verified** (company history; some biographies say he arrived in early 1854, so "1853" is **partly verified**). [Levi Strauss & Co.](https://levistrauss.com/2013/03/14/the-story-of-levi-strauss/)

### 3. Foreign Miners' Taxes

- **1850 act**: $20 a month license for miners who were not U.S. citizens (Californios who became citizens under the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo were exempt). Signed **April 13, 1850**. **Amount verified; date partly verified** (date from Wikipedia and secondary sources; the statute itself is in *Statutes of California* 1850, ch. 97). [Wikipedia summary with statute citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Miners%27_Tax_Act_of_1850)
- **Who it hit**: mainly Mexican, Chilean, Peruvian and other Latin American miners, plus French miners. About 4,000 mostly Mexican and Peruvian miners protested at Sonora, Tuolumne County, on May 19, 1850. **partly verified** (local history and CUNY American Social History Project). [CUNY ASHP, "Latin American and French Miners Protest the Foreign Miner's Tax"](https://shec.ashp.cuny.edu/items/show/1812); [Sonora history](https://sonoraca.com/sonora-stories/foreign-miners-tax-costly-to-sonora/)
- **Repealed March 14, 1851.** **partly verified** (secondary, consistent across sources).
- **1852 act**: $3 a month, passed May 1852 under Governor John Bigler, "explicitly directed at the Chinese miners" (PBS). **verified**. [PBS American Experience, Chinese Immigrants](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/goldrush-chinese-immigrants)
- **Raised to $4 a month on March 30, 1853.** **partly verified** (Santa Clara University exhibit and other secondary sources). Later rates changed again in the mid 1850s; I could not verify the exact 1855 to 1856 rates, so do not show them. [SCU Digital Exhibits](https://dh.scu.edu/exhibits/exhibits/show/california-gold-rush-exhibit/chinese-immigrants/chinese-immigrant-policies)
- By 1870 Chinese miners had paid over $5 million under the tax, "nearly one-quarter of California's state revenue" (PBS). **verified** as PBS's statement.
- **Why "citizens" matters**: U.S. naturalization law limited citizenship to "any Alien being a free white person" (1790 act, still in force in the 1850s). So Chinese miners could never become citizens and stop paying; European immigrants could. **verified**. [Immigration History, Nationality Act of 1790](https://immigrationhistory.org/item/1790-nationality-act/)

### 4. Chinese immigration

- **Arrivals**: 2,716 Chinese arrivals in 1851; **20,026 in 1852**, "following crop failures in southern China." **verified**. [PBS American Experience](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/goldrush-chinese-immigrants). Mae Ngai: "In 1852, the arrivals jumped to more than twenty thousand." [Ngai, History News Network](https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/america-once-was-eager-for-chinese-immigration-mae)
- **Origins**: mostly the Pearl River Delta of Guangdong, sailing from Hong Kong. **verified** (PBS; NCC).
- **Voyage length**: Ngai: "It generally took ten to twelve weeks to sail from Hong Kong to San Francisco." Fast clippers did it in about 40 to 50 days (e.g., *Golden West*, 47 days in 1856). **partly verified**: range is roughly six weeks to three months. The game's 50-day leg in `route.js` is within range.
- **Financing**: Ngai describes "farmers, artisans, and merchants, who mostly paid their own way or borrowed money for the voyage to America." The **credit-ticket** system (a broker advances the fare, repaid from wages) existed, but how common it was in the 1850s is **disputed**. Do not present it as the only way. The Chan family summary ("Your village pooled money and borrowed more") fits the evidence well.
- **"Coolie"**: Governor Bigler's April 23, 1852 special message attacked "Coolies" and claimed Chinese were contract laborers held by "Chinese masters." Historians (Ngai) show the California migrants were mostly free migrants. Treat "coolie" as Bigler's slur and claim, not as a description. **verified** (Bigler date and wording via Ngai, [HNN](https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/181031)).
- **District associations (huiguan)**: Sam Yup (Sanyi) company formed in 1851; Sze Yup (Siyi) company late 1851; more formed in 1852 and after; together known as the Chinese Six Companies (formally the Chinese Consolidated Benevolent Association, 1882). **partly verified** (dates from Chinese Historical Society of Southern California and SCU; NPS confirms "organized... in the 1850s and formally established in 1882"). The Sze Yup Association "welcomed Chinese newcomers at docks, provided housing, employment assistance, and mining outfits" (PBS, **verified**). That the associations tracked members' debts before they could go home is commonly stated by historians (e.g., Gunther Barth) but I did **not** find an authoritative online source: **partly verified**. [NPS, The Chinese Six Companies](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/the-chinese-six-companies.htm); [CHSSC](https://chssc.org/?p=13692)
- **Norman Asing's letter**: response to Bigler's April 23, 1852 message, published in the San Francisco *Daily Alta California*, **early May 1852** (most scholarly sources say May 5; PBS says May 15: **date partly verified**). Asing ran a San Francisco restaurant and was a merchant and community leader. Short exact quote (as transcribed by PBS and consistent with the National Constitution Center excerpt):
  > "The declaration of your independence, and all the acts of your government, your people, and your history are all against you."

  Opening line: "I am a Chinaman, a republican, and a lover of free institutions." **verified** (text; 1852 newspaper, public domain). [PBS, Becoming American](https://www.pbs.org/becomingamerican/ce_witness1.html); [National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/historic-document-library/detail/norman-asing-petition-to-california-governor-john-bigler). The original is in the California Digital Newspaper Collection (UC Riverside), which blocked automated access; a teacher should eyeball the scan before printing.

### 5. People v. Hall (1854)

- **Citation**: *People v. Hall*, 4 Cal. 399 (1854). Opinion by Chief Justice Hugh C. Murray, Justice Solomon Heydenfeldt concurring, Justice Alexander Wells dissenting. Decided October 1854 (CourtListener lists 1854-10-15). **verified** (citation); **partly verified** (exact day). [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5590541/people-v-hall/); [Immigration History](https://immigrationhistory.org/item/people-v-hall/)
- **Facts**: George W. Hall, a white man, was convicted in Nevada County and sentenced to death for murdering **Ling Sing**, a Chinese miner, in 1853. Chinese witnesses testified against him. **verified**. [SFSU Asian American biography project, Ling Sing](https://apiabiography.sfsu.edu/ling-sing)
- **Holding**: The 1850 Criminal Proceedings Act barred testimony by any "black or mulatto person, or Indian" for or against a white man. The court ruled those words were generic racial terms covering all non-white people, so Chinese testimony against a white person was barred. Hall's conviction was reversed. **verified**.
- The opinion called Chinese people "a race of people whom nature has marked as inferior, and who are incapable of progress or intellectual development beyond a certain point." **verified** (quoted by Immigration History). Use only in teacher materials, not on a student card.
- **Effect**: Chinese victims of crimes by white people had almost no legal recourse. The testimony ban was dropped when California adopted its 1872 Penal Code. **partly verified**.
- Black Californians: the same 1850 law barred Black testimony against whites. This supports the `black-california` ending ("its laws still limit Black residents"). **verified**.

### 6. Californios and the Land Act of 1851

- **Act of March 3, 1851** ("An Act to Ascertain and Settle the Private Land Claims in the State of California") required every holder of a Spanish or Mexican grant to prove title before the Board of Land Commissioners. **verified**.
- "The Board heard **813** cases between 1851 and 1856, and in **604** of those cases the titles were confirmed." Most decisions were appealed to federal courts. **verified**.
- "The confirmation process required lawyers, translators, and surveyors, and took an average of **17 years** to resolve." **verified**. [Online Archive of California, Bancroft Library finding aid for the Land Case Files](https://www.oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/hb109nb422/admin/)
- Burden of proof on landholders; legal fees, debts and squatters cost many Californios their land even when titles were confirmed. **partly verified** (OAC finding aid and standard histories). Vallejo "lost most of his land to squatter occupation and expensive legal proceedings" (University of Oklahoma Press description of his *Recuerdos*). **verified**. [OU Press, Recuerdos](https://www.oupress.com/9780806190778/recuerdos)
- **Vallejo quote**: The 1875 Spanish original of his *Recuerdos históricos y personales tocante a la Alta California* (Bancroft Library manuscript) is public domain. As transcribed by Peralta Hacienda Historical Park:
  > "¿a qué bien quejarnos? el mal está hecho y ya no tiene remedio."
  > ("What good is it to complain? The evil is done and can't be undone.")

  The longer passage begins "Ya el pendón de las estrellas ondea en la Alta California" and complains that the new government "había prometido respetar y hacer respetar nuestros derechos." **partly verified**: transcription from a museum audio script, not checked against the manuscript. The English is the museum's translation; the 2023 Beebe and Senkewicz translation is under copyright. Safest: print the Spanish plus the game's own translation, and note the passage is about the U.S. takeover generally, not only land. [Peralta Hacienda audio scripts (PDF)](https://www.peraltahacienda.org/PH_allalbums/audio/scripts/scripts_pll.pdf)

### 7. California Native peoples, 1846 to 1873

- **Population**: "Between 1846 and 1873, California's Indian population plunged from perhaps 150,000 to 30,000." (Madley, *An American Genocide*, Yale 2016, as described by UCLA American Indian Studies Center). **verified**. NMAI's Native Knowledge 360 lesson: "An estimated 150,000 Native Americans lived in California in 1848." **verified**. Earlier estimates by Sherburne F. Cook (UC Berkeley) give the same range for 1848 to 1870. **partly verified** (I did not read Cook's tables directly). [UCLA AISC](https://amindian.ucla.edu/publication/an-american-genocide/); [NMAI NK360, Impact of the Gold Rush on Native Americans of California](https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/resources/Impact-of-the-Gold-Rush-on-Native-Americans-of-California)
- Madley: "Vigilantes, militiamen, and soldiers killed between 9,492 and 16,094 California Indians and probably more" (1846 to 1873); "370 separate massacres"; forcible transfer of 3,000 to 4,000 Indian children, 1850 to 1868; in Los Angeles census counts the Indian population fell from 3,669 (1850) to 219 (1870). **verified** (as Madley's findings). [USC lecture summary](https://dornsife.usc.edu/cagr/2016/10/31/benjamin-madley-lecture-summary/)
- **Act for the Government and Protection of Indians**, passed **April 22, 1850**. It allowed Indian children and adults to be "apprenticed" or indentured to whites, let "vagrant" Indians be hired out to the highest bidder, and barred convicting a white man on an Indian's testimony. Not fully repealed until 1937. **verified**. [Johnston-Dodds, *Early California Laws and Policies Related to California Indians*, California Research Bureau, California State Library, 2002 (PDF)](https://cawaterlibrary.net/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Early-Laws-Pertaining-to-Tribes.pdf)
- **Governor Peter Burnett, annual message, January 7, 1851** (exact):
  > "That a war of extermination will continue to be waged between the races, until the Indian race becomes extinct, must be expected. While we cannot anticipate this result but with painful regret, the inevitable destiny of the race is beyond the power or wisdom of man to avert."

  **verified** (Johnston-Dodds 2002, p. 15, citing "Governor's Annual Message to the Legislature, January 7, 1851," in *Journals of the Senate and Assembly of the State of California*, 1852, p. 15). Note he framed it as "inevitable"; quote the full first sentence, not just "war of extermination."
- **State militia spending (from the State Comptroller's records, California State Archives)**:
  - 1850 to 1852 expeditions: **$843,573.48** total (Mariposa and Monterey $259,372.31; Second El Dorado $199,784.59; Gila $113,482.25; and others). **verified**.
  - Total claims for "Expeditions against the Indians," 1851 to 1859: **$1,293,179.20**. **verified**.
  - 1854 to 1859 expeditions: California submitted $449,605.74 to the U.S.; Congress (Act of March 2, 1861) paid **$229,987.67**. **verified**. (All: Johnston-Dodds 2002, Tables 1 and 2.)
  - For the early (1850 to 1852) expeditions, Congress voted in 1854 to reimburse about **$900,000** (Madley, quoted in the Anchorage Daily News; figures near $924,000 appear elsewhere). **partly verified**: exact amount and act not confirmed in a primary source. Madley also says the legislature passed funding bills totaling about $1.5 million. Safe wording: "The state spent more than $1 million on militia expeditions against Native people, and the federal government paid most of it back." [ADN, 2016](https://www.adn.com/arts/books/2016/09/21/author-of-controversial-book-on-indian-extermination-in-california-speaks-in-anchorage/)
- **Bloody Island, May 15, 1850**, Clear Lake (Lake County): U.S. Army dragoons under Lt. Nathaniel Lyon and Lt. J. W. Davidson killed Pomo people, mostly women, children and old men, after Pomo laborers held by Andrew Kelsey and Charles Stone killed their captors. Death toll estimates range from about 60 to 200 or more (**disputed** number). State landmark No. 427 plaque: "in 1850, U.S. soldiers nearly annihilated the Indian inhabitants for the murder of two white men. Doubt exists of these Indians' guilt." **verified** (plaque text). [California Office of Historic Preservation, Landmark 427](https://ohp.parks.ca.gov/ListedResources/Detail/427)
- **Naming nations accurately** (match place to people):
  - Coloma, American River, Bear and Yuba rivers: **Nisenan** and foothill **Miwok** (State Parks). **verified**.
  - Feather River (Rich Bar, Indian Bar, where Dame Shirley lived): **Maidu** territory. **partly verified**.
  - Clear Lake, Bloody Island: **Pomo**. **verified**.
  - Round Valley, Mendocino County (mass killings in the later 1850s): **Yuki**. **partly verified** (Madley); outside the game's 1849 to 1854 window.
  - The `militia-news` card's Nisenan trader fits the American River and Sacramento-area gold fields.
- **Today**: Governor Newsom formally apologized on June 18, 2019 (Executive Order N-15-19) and created a Truth and Healing Council. California has more federally recognized tribes than any other state. **verified** (apology). [Office of the Governor](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2019/06/18/governor-newsom-issues-apology-to-native-americans-for-states-historical-wrongdoings-establishes-truth-and-healing-council/)

### 8. Mining-camp voices: Dame Shirley and Sarah Royce

- **Louise Amelia Knapp Smith Clappe ("Dame Shirley")** wrote 23 letters from Rich Bar and Indian Bar on the Feather River, 1851 to 1852. They were first published in *The Pioneer* magazine (San Francisco, 1854 to 1855) and are **public domain**. Text below checked against the Project Gutenberg edition (from the 1922 Thomas C. Russell edition). **verified**. [Project Gutenberg #23280](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/23280)
  - Letter the Fifteenth, Indian Bar, April 10, 1852:
    > "Gold-mining is nature's great lottery scheme. A man may work in a claim for many months, and be poorer at the end of the time than when he commenced, or he may take out thousands in a few hours. It is a mere matter of chance."
  - Letter the Sixteenth, Indian Bar, May 1, 1852, on a Rich Bar rule "that no foreigner shall work in the mines on that bar":
    > "It seems to me that the above law is selfish, cruel, and narrow-minded in the extreme."
  - Same letter, a Spanish-speaking miner stabbed by an American for asking to be repaid: "Nothing was done and very little was said about this atrocious affair."
- **Sarah Royce**: her memoir *A Frontier Lady: Recollections of the Gold Rush and Early California* was written decades later (for her son Josiah Royce) and **first published in 1932** by Yale University Press. A 1932 U.S. book is public domain only if its copyright was not renewed; I could **not** confirm its status (no Gutenberg or HathiTrust full-text copy found). **Not verified as public domain. Do not use a Royce quote in the game** unless a teacher confirms the copyright status. Use Clappe instead.

### 9. California statehood (teaser)

- California's 1849 constitution, written at Monterey, declared: "Neither Slavery nor involuntary Servitude, unless for the punishment of crimes, shall ever be tolerated in this State." **verified** (exact wording via multiple legal sources; California State Archives holds the original).
- In late 1849 California applied for admission as a free state, which upset the 15/15 balance of free and slave states and set off the crisis resolved by the **Compromise of 1850**. **verified**.
- "California became the 31st state to join the Union on **September 9, 1850**... as one element of the hotly contested Compromise of 1850." **verified**. [U.S. Senate, California](https://www.senate.gov/states/CA/intro.htm)

## PART B: Game design and pitfalls

### 1. The Oregon Trail (Gameloft, 2021)

- Released on Apple Arcade April 2, 2021. Gameloft brought in three Indigenous historians: **Margaret Huettl** (University of Nebraska-Lincoln), **Katrina Phillips** (Macalester College), and **William Bauer** (UNLV). **verified** (NPR/KPBS; UNL News). [NPR via KPBS, May 12, 2021](https://www.kpbs.org/news/national/2021/05/12/a-new-spin-on-a-classic-video-game-gives-native-americans-better-representation); [UNL News](https://news.unl.edu/newsrooms/today/article/huettls-oregon-trail-work-featured-in-science-caf-series)
- Changes reported: playable Native characters with their own storylines; removing braids as a universal Native look; replacing bows and arrows with period-accurate rifles; less stereotyped "drum and flute" music; Native characters speaking fluent English (reflecting real multilingualism). **verified** (NPR).
- Opening message reported as: "For Indigenous Peoples, westward expansion was not an adventure but an invasion." **partly verified** (secondary report).
- Creative director Jarrad Trudgen on Native perspectives: "as a white, middle-class Australian, I don't think I can really speak to that. I'd like help with that." **verified** (NPR).
- Lesson: consultants changed costume, weapons, sound and voice, not only text. Consultation needs to start early, and someone with authority to change things must act on it.

### 2. Mission US

- **"A Cheyenne Odyssey"**: "the team met with representatives of the Northern Cheyenne Tribe at Chief Dull Knife College... who later consulted on educational content, scripting, design, and casting for the game." Northern Cheyenne roles were voiced by Northern Cheyenne actors; Richard Littlebear (president, Chief Dull Knife College) was an advisor. **verified**. [Mission US, Process and Development](https://www.mission-us.org/about/creating-mission-us/process-and-development/); [Credits](https://www.mission-us.org/about/our-team/credits/credits-a-cheyenne-odyssey/)
- Its classroom content advisory says the game "does not directly depict graphic violence" and "more prominently feature[s] the perspective of the Northern Cheyenne." Teacher tips: "Preview the issue with your students... Set ground rules for classroom talk. Debrief and discuss episodes where violence occurs." **verified**. [Mission 3 Content Advisory (PDF)](https://assets.mission-us.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/16154403/mission_3_classroom_content_advisory_finalrev.pdf)
- **Research**: EDC's 2009 field test (387 students, grades 5 to 9) found gains in knowledge and document-analysis skill; a 2016 EDC study of "Up from the Dust" found "a 15% knowledge gain from pretest to posttest" versus under 1% for controls. I found **no published outcome study specific to A Cheyenne Odyssey**. **verified** (as stated). [Mission US, Research and Outcomes](https://mission-us.org/about/proven-impact/research-and-outcomes/)
- **"Flight to Freedom" backlash**: the brief says 2019, but the documented controversies were **2015** (a social-media campaign led by educator Rafranz Davis) and **October 2017** (Phoenix Elementary School District blocked the game after parents objected). I found no 2019 event. **disputed (date in brief is wrong)**. ASU professor Neal A. Lester: "Why does it have to be fun? Slavery wasn't fun." **partly verified** (KJZZ, public radio). [KJZZ, Oct 2017](https://kjzz.org/content/550387/asu-professor-discusses-criticism-over-slavery-computer-game); [The Root](https://www.theroot.com/slavery-simulation-game-outrages-parents-in-phoenix)
- Lessons: (a) the backlash centered on putting children in the role of a person suffering oppression, with game mechanics (scores, failure states) attached to that suffering; (b) the Phoenix district said it was "unsure how the game got into the classroom," so teacher preparation and parent notice matter as much as content.

### 3. Portraying Native peoples in classroom simulations

- ADL, "Why Simulation Activities Should Not Be Used": simulations "trivialize the experience of the victims," "stereotype group behavior and distort historical reality," "can put students in the position of defending and/or identifying with the oppressors," and "can be emotionally upsetting." Recommends primary sources, case studies, reflective writing. **verified**. [ADL](https://www.adl.org/resources/tools-and-strategies/why-simulation-activities-should-not-be-used)
- Learning for Justice, "Classroom Simulations: Proceed With Caution" (2008): Facing History's Phredd Matthews Wall warns against the message "Now you know what it feels like to be..." **verified**. [Learning for Justice archive](https://archive.learningforjustice.org/magazine/spring-2008/classroom-simulations-proceed-with-caution)
- On Native history specifically, Meredith McCoy (Carleton College), in K-12 Dive (Oct 2024), on simulations and reenactments: "These do tangible harm and naturalize the context as normal. They're counterproductive, and these are stereotypes that just aren't real." **verified**. [K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/ensure-native-americans-accurately-represented-curriculum/730024)
- NMAI Native Knowledge 360 Essential Understandings: "There is no single American Indian culture or language"; Native nations are present-day nations, not only historical. **verified**. [Smithsonian Magazine, Sept 18, 2019](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/inside-new-effort-change-what-schools-teach-about-native-american-history-180973166/)
- NMAI has a ready-made inquiry lesson for grades 8 to 12: "Do American actions against California Native Americans during the gold rush meet the United Nations definition of genocide?" Its teacher tips: acknowledge the topic "is difficult and can be emotional," and end with Native responses showing tribal nations "persevered." **verified**. [NK360 Gold Rush lesson](https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/resources/Impact-of-the-Gold-Rush-on-Native-Americans-of-California)

### 4. Co-located group play around one screen

These are design recommendations drawn from research on "single display groupware" and cooperative learning, not verified historical facts.
- Research on shared displays (Stewart, Bederson and Druin, CHI 1999, University of Maryland) found children were more engaged with more than one input device; one mouse tends to let one student dominate. Westward's rotating "lead role holds the mouse" is a good fix. [UMD HCIL paper (PDF)](https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/hcil/jazz/learn/papers/chi-98-sdg.pdf)
- Give each role a non-mouse job every turn (reading aloud, tracking money on paper, writing the journal line) so that three of four students are never idle.
- Make votes visible and quick (show of hands, then one click) and show whose turn it is in large type readable from 2 meters.
- Keep text readable aloud in under 30 seconds per card; one reader, not four people reading silently at different speeds.
- Build in a pause point for debrief (the teacher can freeze all screens), and plan for the end ledger to be discussed as a whole class.

## CORRECTIONS to the game's draft text

Timeline note first: overland families start 1849-05-01 and reach California around autumn 1849. The Chan cousins start 1852-03-01 and, with the route's 50 + 10 + 4 days, reach the mines around early May 1852. Several cards mix these dates.

1. **data/cards.js, `making-a-living`**: "A shovel costs ten times what it did back east." The exact multiplier is not verified. Fix: "Boots, shovels, and food cost many times what they did back east."
2. **data/cards.js, `californio-rancho`**: In autumn 1849 the Land Act did not exist yet (March 3, 1851). Fix text: "Don Ignacio, a Californio ranchero, watches squatters build cabins on land his family has held for decades. Soon a federal law, the Land Act of 1851, will make him prove his title before a land commission and the courts. With appeals, cases took 17 years on average." Keep the choice results.
3. **data/cards.js, `militia-news`**: California had no state government until December 1849 and the first state militia expeditions were in 1850. Fix text: start with "By 1850, miners in camp talk about militia companies paid by the state to attack Native villages in the foothills." The Nisenan trader is fine for the American River area; label the "moved three times" detail as a composite, not a documented person.
4. **data/cards.js, `cross-chan-tax`** (shown to overland families, who arrive in 1849): the anti-Chinese $3 tax dates from May 1852, and few Chinese miners were in the mines in 1849 to 1850. Fix: year-stamp the card "1852" (e.g., "Three years later, in 1852, a tax collector rides up to a camp of Chinese miners downstream...") or, for an 1850 card, use the $20 tax on Mexican and Chilean miners instead.
5. **data/cards.js, `tax-collector`**: "Every foreign miner must pay a monthly license tax. American-born miners pay nothing." is wrong: naturalized immigrants were exempt, and the reason Chinese miners could never stop paying was the "free white person" naturalization rule. Fix: "A tax collector rides into camp. Since May 1852, every miner who is not a U.S. citizen must buy a license for $3 a month. ## MYTHS / pitfalls to avoid

- **"The 1850 Foreign Miners' Tax targeted the Chinese."** False. Few Chinese were in California in 1850; the $20 tax fell mainly on Mexican, Chilean, Peruvian and French miners. The anti-Chinese tax is the 1852 one. (Some web exhibits, including a university one, get this wrong.)
- **"Coolies."** This was Governor Bigler's charge in April 1852. Most Chinese gold seekers were free migrants who paid or borrowed for passage. Do not use "coolie" in student-facing text except inside a quoted primary source.
- **"Levi Strauss got rich selling jeans to forty-niners."** He arrived in 1853 to sell dry goods; the riveted jeans patent is from 1873.
- **"300,000 people came in 1849."** The 300,000 figure is for roughly 1848 to 1855. About 100,000 non-Natives were in California by the end of 1849.
- **"Chinese could not testify at all."** People v. Hall barred testimony against white people. It came in 1854, not 1852.
- **"California's Native people died mostly of disease."** Disease, starvation and displacement mattered, but historians document deliberate killing and state funding for it. Avoid both "it was just disease" and unverified claims such as "the state paid scalp bounties" (bounties were offered by some towns and private groups; the state paid militia expenses).
- **Burnett's quote cut short.** Quote the full sentence ("...must be expected") and note that he called it "inevitable."
- **Bloody Island numbers.** Estimates range from about 60 to 200 or more. Say "dozens to more than a hundred" or cite the range.
- **Native nations as interchangeable.** Coloma and the American River: Nisenan and foothill Miwok. Feather River: Maidu. Clear Lake: Pomo. Do not put a Pomo character on the American River.
- **Copyright traps.** Sarah Royce's *A Frontier Lady* (1932) may still be in copyright. Modern English translations of Vallejo (2023) are copyrighted; use the 1875 Spanish with your own translation.
- **The "Flight to Freedom" date.** The controversies were 2015 and 2017, not 2019.
- **The NMAI American Indian Magazine article** says colonists grew to "more than 300,000" by 1849. That conflicts with the other sources; do not reuse it.

## Design implications for Westward

1. **Never make Native suffering a mechanic.** No card should give players points, gold or speed for anything done to Native people, and no choice should let students play as a militia. The `militia-news` card handles this correctly: the family hears and records; nothing is scored. Keep it that way.
2. **Do not put students in the role of Native characters suffering violence.** Westward's four playable families are all newcomers, which avoids the "Flight to Freedom" problem. Native people appear as named neighbors with their own voices (the Nisenan trader, the Shoshone guide), and the end ledger speaks for "the people already here." Keep Native voices in the present tense in the ledger (e.g., "Nisenan and Miwok people still live in the Sierra foothills today"), per NMAI.
3. **The Chan cousins are the one oppressed playable family.** Their hardships (tax, robbery, no testimony) should be shown as unjust laws, not as bad luck to beat. Use flat consequences, no "score" for coping, and pair each with a voice of resistance (Asing's letter). This follows the ADL and Learning for Justice cautions.
4. **Stamp years on cards.** California cards span 1849 to 1854. A small year label ("1850", "1852", "1854") fixes most of the anachronisms above and teaches change over time.
5. **Primary voices instead of narration.** Use Clappe's two quotes (lottery; "selfish, cruel, and narrow-minded") for the overland families, Asing for the Chans, Vallejo (Spanish plus translation) for the rancho card, and Burnett only in the teacher debrief, not on a card.
6. **Teacher prep and parent notice.** Ship a one-page content advisory like Mission US's ("Preview... Set ground rules... Debrief"). The Phoenix case shows that a game used without preparation becomes the story.
7. **Debrief, not "now you know how it felt."** End with the "Who Decided?" ledger and the NMAI inquiry question (does what happened meet the UN definition of genocide?) as an optional follow-up lesson, rather than implying students experienced it.
8. **Group play.** Keep the rotating mouse, give every role a job off the mouse, and keep card text short enough to read aloud.
