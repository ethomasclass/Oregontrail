# Westward: trail and Oregon fact check

Research pass for the overland trail half of the game (Independence to Fort Hall, the Oregon branch, and the trail facts behind the California branch cards). Lesson window: 1849 to 1854.

Confidence key:
- **verified**: confirmed in an authoritative source I read directly (page text or original scan).
- **partly verified**: confirmed only through a secondary source, a search snippet of an authoritative page I could not open, or sources disagree on the exact number.
- **disputed**: historians disagree, or the claim as worded is not supported.

Access notes: oregonencyclopedia.org and octa-trails.org pages blocked automated reading (HTTP 403 or a Cloudflare check). Where I cite them, I am relying on the text shown in search results, and the item is marked partly verified unless another source confirmed it. loc.gov classroom pages were also blocked; LOC facts come from search-result text of loc.gov pages.

Machine-readable list: `research/trail-sources.json` (ids match the game's `sources` fields).

## 1. Manifest Destiny (O'Sullivan, 1845)

- **Exact wording (verified against the 1845 scan):** "...checking the fulfilment of our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions." The game poster's excerpt, "our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions", matches the original word for word. Note the original spells it "fulfilment", which falls outside the game's excerpt anyway.
  - Publication: "Annexation," *The United States Magazine and Democratic Review*, Vol. 17, No. 85 (July-August 1845), p. 5. Confidence: verified.
  - Sources: original scan, Internet Archive item `sim_united-states-democratic-review_july-august-1845_17_85` (https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-democratic-review_july-august-1845_17_85); transcription at Teaching American History (https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/annexation/).
- **Authorship (disputed):** The essay was unsigned. It has traditionally been credited to editor John L. O'Sullivan. In 1999 historian Linda S. Hudson argued that journalist Jane McManus Storm (later Cazneau) wrote it; O'Sullivan's biographer Robert Sampson disputes this. "John L. O'Sullivan, 1845" is the standard attribution, but a footnote or teacher note could say "usually credited to." Source: Hudson dissertation, UNT Digital Library (https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278486/m2/1/high_res_d/1002658825-hudson.pdf). Confidence: disputed (attribution only).

## 2. How many people went west, and where

- **About 300,000 to 400,000 overland emigrants, 1840 to 1860.** The Oregon Encyclopedia gives "300,000 to 400,000"; the NPS "Death and Danger on the Emigrant Trails" page uses "an estimated 350,000." Confidence: verified (range). Sources: https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/death-on-trails.htm ; https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/oregon_trail (snippet).
- **Unruh's count (The Plains Across, 1979), 1840 to 1860:** Oregon 53,062; California about 200,000; Utah 42,862; total 296,259. Unruh's count is lower than the round "400,000" because he tallied only these three destinations and was conservative. Confidence: partly verified (Unruh's table as reprinted by BLM, citing William E. Hill). Source: BLM "Overland Trails in Numbers" handout, https://www.blm.gov/sites/default/files/docs/2024-01/Overland%20Trail%20in%20Numbers%20(Upper%20Elementary).final_.pdf
- **Year by year in the game's window (Unruh via BLM):** 1849: Oregon 450, California 25,000, Utah 1,500. 1850: Oregon 6,000, California 44,000. 1851: Oregon 3,600, California 1,100. 1852: Oregon 10,000, California 50,000, Utah 10,000. 1853: Oregon 7,500, California 20,000. 1854: Oregon 6,000, California 12,000. Confidence: partly verified (same source). NPS separately confirms "25,000" for California in 1849 and an estimated 140,000 emigrants arriving in California by the trail from 1849 to 1854 (https://www.nps.gov/cali/learn/historyculture/california-gold-rush.htm). Verified.
- **Teaching point for the game:** In 1849, the year all three trail families leave, Oregon drew only about 450 overland emigrants while about 25,000 headed for California. An Oregon-bound family in 1849 was unusual. The game is not wrong, but the fork card should say that most wagons that year turned toward gold.
- **The Great Migration of 1843:** nearly 1,000 people left Independence for Oregon in spring 1843. Verified. Source: https://www.nps.gov/oreg/learn/historyculture/index.htm

## 3. Outfitting costs (around 1849)

- **Yoke of oxen:** in the late 1840s a yoke cost from about $25 to $65. Joseph Ware's 1849 *Emigrant's Guide to California* priced four yoke at $50 a yoke ($200). The game's $50 a yoke is inside the documented range. Confidence: partly verified (Unruh figure via secondary sources; Ware figure via Historic Oregon City). Sources: https://historicoregoncity.org/2019/04/03/outfitting-for-the-journey/ ; California State Parks wagon-train lesson (https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/485/files/wagon%20train%20revised%203-12-12.pdf, search snippet).
- **How many oxen:** Ware's guide recommended four yoke for a wagon; the game's store text says "at least two yoke." Three or four yoke was the common advice. Confidence: partly verified.
- **Wagon:** "The average wagon cost approximately $85 and the cover around $100" (NPS wording; the cover figure looks high and may mean wagon plus cover). Ware lists "one wagon with cover" at $100. Confidence: partly verified. Source: https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/traveling-emigrant-trails.htm
- **Whole outfit:** "In 1850, it cost roughly $800 to $1,200 to obtain a proper outfit and get by for a whole year" (NPS). Other secondary sources give $800 to $1,000 for a family of four. Confidence: verified (NPS) for the range. Source: https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/traveling-emigrant-trails.htm
- **Food per adult (verified against the 1845 original):** Lansford Hastings advised that "the emigrant should provide himself with, at least, two hundred pounds of flour, or meal; one hundred and fifty pounds of bacon; ten pounds of coffee; twenty pounds of sugar; and ten pounds of salt." *The Emigrants' Guide to Oregon and California* (1845), pp. 143 to 144. Source: https://archive.org/details/emigrantsguideto04hast
  - For a family of four that is about 800 lb of flour and 600 lb of bacon. The game's "recommended" amounts (6 sacks of flour = 600 lb, 6 x 50 lb of bacon = 300 lb) are below Hastings's figure. This is a game-balance choice, but the store intro should not imply that this is what the guidebooks said.
- **Flour and bacon unit prices in Independence, 1849:** not verified. For comparison, Amelia Knight bought flour at $3.50 per hundred pounds near Council Bluffs in May 1853 (Transactions of the Oregon Pioneer Association, 1928 reunion volume, p. 40), so the game's $4 per 100 lb sack is plausible. Confidence: partly verified (plausible only).

## 4. Trip length, mileage, season

- **Distance:** about 2,000 miles from the Missouri River to the Willamette Valley (NPS and Historic Oregon City: Oregon City was "not quite 2000 miles from Independence"). The often-quoted figure of 2,170 miles comes from Wikipedia. To Sacramento, about 1,950 to 2,000 miles. Confidence: partly verified (sources round differently). Sources: https://historicoregoncity.org/2019/04/03/oregon-trail-mileposts/ ; https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/traveling-emigrant-trails.htm
- **Time on the road:** NPS says 4 to 6 months, typically 12 to 15 miles a day. OCTA's summary of Unruh gives average trip lengths of 129.0 days to Oregon and 131.6 days to California in 1849, and 139.6 days to Oregon and 121.0 to California averaged over 1841 to 1860. Confidence: verified (NPS); partly verified (OCTA/Unruh averages, read from a search snippet). Sources: https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/traveling-emigrant-trails.htm ; https://octa-trails.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/793_02-How-long-was-the-journey-from-Missouri-More-Trail-Facts.pdf
- **Departure season:** emigrants left "when the grass started to green, typically mid April" (NPS), with most companies leaving in April and May. The game's May 1, 1849 start is realistic. Verified.
- **Independence Rock by July 4th:** "They felt well on their way if they could reach Independence Rock by the Fourth of July." The name comes from William Sublette's party, who "kept the 4th of July" there in 1830. Pierre-Jean De Smet called it "the great register of the desert" (1841). Verified. Source: WyoHistory.org, https://www.wyohistory.org/essays/independence-rock
- **Chimney Rock:** rises more than 300 feet and "was mentioned in more emigrant diaries than any other landmark on the Oregon Trail." Verified. Source: https://www.nps.gov/places/000/chimney-rock-national-historic-site.htm

- **The split (trail-split):** The Oregon and California trails ran together about 50 miles southwest from Fort Hall to a second "parting of the ways" at the Raft River. Many 1849 gold seekers never saw Fort Hall at all: Hudspeth's Cutoff (opened 1849) left the main trail west of Soda Springs and bypassed the fort, and the Sublette-Greenwood Cutoff (1844) skipped Fort Bridger. Confidence: partly verified (Wikipedia California Trail and Route of the Oregon Trail articles; confirm with NPS California NHT maps before publishing). Placing the vote "at Fort Hall" is an acceptable simplification if the text says "past Fort Hall."
- **Barlow Road (barlow-road), verified:** The Barlow party left The Dalles on September 24, 1845; Sam Barlow cut the road in spring 1846 and charged "$5 toll per wagon plus 10 cents per head of livestock." In the first season "a thousand emigrants, 152 wagons" used it. Later travelers took "about six days" (the game's 7 days is fine). It ran along the south flank of Mount Hood and down the very steep Laurel Hill. The toll was "much cheaper than boat passage on the Columbia River." Source: NPS, https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/the-barlow-road.htm
- **Columbia River route, verified:** Before the Barlow Road, wagons could go no farther than The Dalles. Families rafted or boated down the Columbia, and "most overlanders enlisted the help of Indigenous people to navigate the river"; "nearly all overlanders in 1843 paid Chinookan people for help circumventing the rapids" at the Cascades. Source: NPS, https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/overlanders-in-the-columbia-river-gorge-1840-1870-a-narrative-history.htm
- **Forty Mile Desert (forty-mile-desert), verified:** "a barren stretch of waterless alkali wasteland... the most dreaded section of the California Emigrant Trail." Travelers often crossed at night. An 1850 survey counted about 1,061 dead mules, 5,000 horses, 3,750 cattle, and 953 graves. Routes forked to the Truckee River (1844) or the Carson River (1848). Source: Nevada State Historic Preservation Office marker No. 26, https://shpo.nv.gov/nevadas-historical-markers/historical-markers/forty-mile-desert

## 6. River crossings, ferries, and Native tolls

- **Papin (Pappan) ferry (kansas-river-ferries):** Started in the early 1840s (sources say 1842 or 1843) at present Topeka by the brothers Joseph, Ahcan, and Louis Pappan, French-Canadian traders whose wives were daughters of a French trader and a Kanza (Kaw) mother. The 1843 boat was three dugout canoes under a deck, and they "charged $1 per wagon." By 1849 they ran two boats, each carrying two wagons. Confidence: partly verified (Friends of the Kaw at kansasriver.org and Legends of Kansas, both drawing on Kansas Historical Society material). Sources: https://kansasriver.org/learn/life-on-the-kaw/the-working-kaw/ ; https://legendsofkansas.com/pappans-ferry-topeka-kansas/
- **Uniontown crossing, 1849:** "Thousands of emigrants bypassed Pappan's Ferry at Topeka and headed 15 miles west" to Uniontown, where at least two ferries carried 65 to 70 wagons a day, "at $1 per wagon." Confidence: partly verified (same source).
- **Shawnee and Delaware ferries, verified:** Kansas Historical Quarterly (George A. Root, 1934) quotes a member of Doniphan's 1846 expedition: "The Shawnee and Delaware tribes of Indians have settled here... They keep a ferry boat here, in which we crossed the river. The keeper of the boat said he had made four hundred dollars this season by the crossing of emigrants bound to Oregon." A Shawnee man named Toley ran a ferry near Grinter's crossing. Source: https://www.kansashistory.gov/p/ferries-in-kansas-part-ii-kansas-river/17493
- **Ferry prices elsewhere (for comparison, verified from Knight's 1853 diary):** $3 a wagon at the Loup Fork (Nebraska); $3 a wagon on a bridge over the Sweetwater; $8 a wagon plus $1 a head at the Green River ferry. So $8 is a real price, but for the Green River in 1853, not the Kansas River in 1849.
- **Native tolls (native-tolls):** Knight, May 7, 1853, in Nebraska: "crossed a small creek, with a narrow Indian bridge across it. Paid the Indian 75 cents toll." Verified (original 1928 Transactions text). A Pawnee band at the Loup Fork collected tolls from emigrants (partly verified; BYU Saints by Sea article, https://saintsbysea.byu.edu/static/articles/DevelopmentsAlongTheOverlandTrailFromTheMissouri.pdf, via search snippet). NPS: "Before white men set up ferries and bridges to cross treacherous rivers, Indians were making ferries out of canoes to take wagons and people across." Verified (death-on-trails page).
- **Native trade and guiding (shoshone-trade, native-guides):**
  - NPS (Hagerman Fossil Beds): "The Shoshoneans were welcoming, trading fish and other resources with the weary emigrants." Verified. https://www.nps.gov/hafo/learn/historyculture/index.htm
  - NPS (death-on-trails): Native people "often traded with emigrants," offering fresher food and "moccasins to replace worn out boots." Verified.
  - Knight bought "some fresh salmon from the Indians" in the Grande Ronde Valley, Aug. 17, 1853, and her husband traded hard crackers to Lakota (Sioux) people for moccasins near Fort Laramie, June 7, 1853. Verified (original text).
  - Robert Haldane Renshaw, 1851, at Three Island Crossing on the Snake River: "This morning we hired an Indian to show us the ford." Verified (BLM/OCTA Idaho backcountry byway booklet, p. 10, https://www.blm.gov/sites/blm.gov/files/OregonTrail-Booklet.pdf).
  - Horses: the Fort Hall Shoshone and Bannock kept large horse herds, and emigrants traded for horses there. Partly verified (Idaho State Historical Society reference series, search snippet). Salmon trading belongs on the Snake River (west of Fort Hall), not at South Pass.

## 7. Deaths on the trail

- **Overall:** NPS: "Of the estimated 350,000 who started the journey, disease may have claimed as many as 30,000 victims"; "Nearly one in ten emigrants who set off on the trail did not survive." Unruh estimated about 10,000 deaths 1840 to 1860, closer to 4 percent; Merrill Mattes estimated about 6 percent. Confidence: disputed (the range is wide; 4 to 10 percent). Sources: https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/death-on-trails.htm ; KC Clay, "Emigrants and Death along the Overland Trail" (2014 graduate paper hosted by NPS History eLibrary, citing Unruh pp. 345, 408), https://www.npshistory.com/publications/emigrants-death-overland-trail.pdf
- **Disease was the top killer (cholera-deaths), verified:** "the number one killer, by a wide margin, was disease." Cholera "resulted in more illness and death than all of the other maladies," spread through "polluted, stagnant water," and victims "often died within 12 hours of the first symptoms." The camphor and laudanum emigrants carried "did little to cure the disease." Unruh judged that about 9 in 10 emigrant deaths were from disease. Source: NPS death-on-trails page; Unruh via Clay paper.
- **Cholera years:** cholera hit the Platte River road from 1849 through 1852, worst in 1850 and 1852. The Clay paper, citing Unruh, gives total trail deaths of about 2,100 in 1850 and 2,800 in 1852. Confidence: partly verified. Sources: Clay paper (above); Kansas Historical Society Kansapedia "Cholera" (https://www.kansashistory.gov/kansapedia/cholera/12010, snippet).
- **Accidents (trail-accidents), verified:** "Shootings, drownings, being crushed by wagon wheels, and injuries from handling domestic animals were the common killers on the trail. Wagon accidents were the most prevalent. Both children and adults sometimes fell off or under wagons and were crushed under the wheels." Guns: "almost every wagon was a rolling arsenal. Accidental shootings were common, but murders were rare." Source: NPS death-on-trails page.
- **Drownings:** common at river crossings (NPS list of main causes). Knight saw a returning train whose leader "had been drowned a few days before, in a river called Elkhorn" (May 6, 1853). Verified.
- **Emigrant and Native violence (unruh-violence), verified:** Unruh's "very tentative figures, which are probably low": 362 emigrants killed by Indians and 426 Indians killed by emigrants, 1840 to 1860 (*The Plains Across*, p. 185, note 52 on p. 457). About 90 percent of emigrants killed by Indians died west of South Pass, mostly along the Snake and Humboldt rivers and the Applegate Trail. Unruh found only four well-documented pre-Civil War wagon-train massacres (1852, 1854, 1857, 1860). Sources: OCTA "More Trail Facts" sheet (PDF read directly), https://www.octa-trails.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/801_05-On-what-part-of-the-Oregon-Trail-were-Indian-risks-More-Trail-Facts.pdf ; Gateway Arch National Park museum text, https://archpark.org/visit/points-of-interest/museum-at-the-gateway-arch/manifest-destiny/Conflict-on-the-Trail
- NPS: "American Indians were usually among the least of the emigrants' problems. They were mostly peaceful and helped the emigrants in a variety of ways." "Tales of hostile encounters far overshadowed actual incidents." Verified.

## 8. Emigrant impact on Native homelands (emigrant-impact, bison-decline)

- **Grass, timber, game:** George Bird Grinnell, *The Fighting Cheyennes* (1915, public domain), as quoted in Andrew Isenberg, *The Destruction of the Bison* (Cambridge University Press, 2000): "grass, wood, and game had been so nearly destroyed on the Oregon Trail up the Platte" by the gold rush years; "Famous groves of cottonwood trees where the Indians had camped in winter for generations disappeared in a single season." Isenberg: in the 1840s and 1850s more than 300,000 emigrants and traders crossed the Central Plains, the bison's winter range, and the damage helped drive bison away from the Platte. Confidence: partly verified (read via search text of the Isenberg PDF, https://buffalofieldcampaign.org/images/get-involved/students-resource-about-bison/bison-conservation-papers/Isenberg-The-Destruction-of-the-Bison-An-Environmental-History-2000.pdf).
- **Eyewitness, 1852 (verified, original text):** Caleb and Alice Richey, letter from near Fort Laramie, June 20, 1852: "have seen no buffalo" because "the first emigration scared them off the road." (Transactions of the 40th Annual Reunion of the Oregon Pioneer Association, 1912, p. 590 area; https://archive.org/details/transactionsofan40oreg). Note: the 1852 newspaper editor said these letters were "slightly abridged and corrected in diction."
- **Bison decline, verified:** NPS: "the bison population plummeted from 30-60 million (estimates vary) to fewer than 1,000 animals by the 1890s," driven by "Railways, rifles, and an international market for buffalo hides" and the Army's policy of destroying bison to control Plains nations. William Hornaday's 1889 Smithsonian census counted 1,091. The game's "Within forty years [of 1849], the great herds would be nearly gone" is accurate. Sources: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/bison/people.htm ; Hornaday 1889 via American Yawp Reader (https://www.americanyawp.com/reader/?p=393).
- **Hunting for sport:** NPS: "Hunting was a popular pastime on wagon trains, not just for food, but sport as well." Verified. The game's "and left them" (wasting the carcass) is plausible but I did not find a source that states it directly.
- **Kansas River valley:** a Native elder recalled the river bottoms near Smith's Ferry covered with emigrant wagons (Kansas Historical Quarterly, 1934). Partly verified (quote truncated in my copy).

## 9. Hastings Cutoff and the Donner Party (donner-party)

- Lansford Hastings promoted his cutoff in 1846; he "estimated [it] could save 300 to 400 miles," but it "turned out to be over 100 miles longer than the original California Trail" (NPS also gives 125 miles). It crossed the Wasatch Mountains and the Great Salt Lake Desert. Verified. Source: https://www.nps.gov/places/hastings-cutoff.htm
- The Donner-Reed Party left Fort Bridger 11 days behind Hastings's first groups, lost time finding the route, reached Truckee Lake below the Sierra crest in October 1846, and was trapped for about four months. Of 87 people, about 40 died (counts vary from 39 to 42). Verified (NPS uses 87 and 40). Source: https://home.nps.gov/cali/learn/historyculture/donner-reed-party.htm
- The game card's "swears his cutoff saves 300 miles" fits Hastings's own claim. Verified.

## 10. Oregon, race, and land

- **1844 exclusion ("lash") law (oregon-exclusion-laws):** Passed by the Provisional Government's legislative committee in June 1844 (Oregon State Archives says June 18; other sources say June 26). Peter Burnett, a Missouri emigrant of 1843, introduced it. It banned slavery but ordered free Black adults to leave (men within two years, women within three) on pain of whipping, "thirty-nine lashes, repeated every six months." On December 19, 1844 the lash was replaced with forced public labor followed by removal. The law was repealed July 3, 1845; no whipping is documented. Confidence: verified (NPS confirms June 1844 and Burnett's role; Oregon Secretary of State archives chronology via search snippet for the December 1844 and July 1845 dates). Sources: NPS, https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/chapter-1-race-slavery-and-freedom-the-nature-of-freedom-in-the-west.htm ; Oregon State Archives chronology, https://sos.oregon.gov/archives/exhibits/black-history/Pages/context/chronology.aspx
- **1849 exclusion law:** passed by the Territorial Legislature on September 21, 1849, making it "unlawful for any negro or mulatto to enter into, or reside" in Oregon, with an exception for those already living there. NPS: it "required black and mulatto newcomers to leave the territory within 40 days." Jacob Vanderpool was expelled under it in 1851. Repealed 1854. Confidence: verified (NPS, two pages) for the law and wording; partly verified for the date and the repeal (secondary sources). Sources: NPS (above); NPS "African Americans on the Oregon Trail," https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/african-americans-on-the-oregon-trail.htm
  - **Timing matters for the game:** between July 1845 and September 21, 1849 Oregon had no exclusion law in force. A Black family arriving in fall 1849 would fall under the new law only if they arrived after September 21.
- **1857 constitution:** On November 9, 1857, white male voters approved the constitution (7,195 to 3,195), rejected slavery (7,727 to 2,645), and approved Black exclusion (8,640 to 1,081). It took effect with statehood in 1859, making Oregon the only free state admitted with an exclusion clause. Verified. Source: Oregon History Project, "A New Legal Landscape," https://www.oregonhistoryproject.org/articles/a-new-legal-landscape/
- **Donation Land Claim Act (donation-land-claim), verified:** Signed and effective September 27, 1850. Settlers already living in Oregon by December 1, 1850 could claim 320 acres if single and 640 acres if married, with half held in the wife's name, after four years of living on and farming the land. Settlers arriving from December 1, 1850 to December 1, 1853 (extended to 1855) got 160 acres (320 for a married couple). It was limited to white settlers and "American half-breed Indians"; Black and Hawaiian settlers were excluded, and Native people, who were not citizens, could not claim land. Sources: HistoryLink, https://www.historylink.org/File/9501 ; HistoryLink, https://historylink.org/File/400 ; Oregon History Project (above); NPS African Americans page.
  - Immigrants such as the Irish: the Act covered citizens and, I believe, people who had declared their intention to become citizens. I could not confirm that clause from a source I read directly; check the statute (9 Stat. 496, section 4) before stating it in the game. Confidence: partly verified.
  - For 1849 arrivals, claims were first made under the Provisional Government's land law and then confirmed under the 1850 Act.
- **George Washington Bush (george-bush), verified:** Born around 1790 (some accounts say 10 or more years earlier). In 1844 he and his friend Michael T. Simmons led their families and three others over the Oregon Trail. Oregon's exclusion law barred Bush, so the party settled north of the Columbia; in fall 1845 they founded the settlement that became Tumwater, the first American settlement north of the Columbia, and Bush farmed on what became Bush Prairie. In the 1852 grain shortage Bush "gave out nearly all his crop" to neighbors: "Pay me in kind next year." Because the 1850 Act was for whites only, Congress passed a special act in 1855 to confirm his land. He died April 5, 1863. Sources: HistoryLink, https://historylink.org/file/5645 ; HistoryLink Tumwater, https://historylink.org/File/22514 ; NPS African Americans page (which calls his mother Irish and his father Black).
  - Note: in 1845 the land north of the Columbia was under joint U.S. and British occupation, and the Hudson's Bay Company was the main power there. After the 1846 treaty it was part of Oregon Territory until Washington Territory was created in 1853. So in 1849 the 1849 exclusion law also applied north of the river; it was just harder to enforce there.
- **Peter Burnett:** elected captain of the 1843 wagon train but resigned after eight days; introduced the 1844 lash law; later the first elected governor of California (1849 to 1851), where he again sought Black exclusion. Partly verified (Oregon Encyclopedia and Missouri Encyclopedia via search snippets). Sources: https://oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/burnett_peter/ ; https://missouriencyclopedia.org/people/burnett-peter-hardeman
- **Black emigrants overall:** those reaching Oregon from the 1840s to the 1860s "probably numbered in the hundreds," some free, some enslaved. The 1860 census counted 128 Black residents in Oregon. Verified (NPS) / partly verified (census figure, secondary). Source: NPS African Americans page.

## 11. Free papers in Missouri (free-papers-missouri)

- Missouri's March 14, 1835 law required free Black residents to obtain a county court license to remain in the state. NPS: "State law also required free Blacks already residing in Missouri to carry a license; otherwise, they could be fined or imprisoned as runaway slaves." Missouri passed laws in 1835, 1843, and 1847 barring free Black people from entering. "Slave catchers operating along the Missouri border... [captured] Black people and [sold] them into bondage, regardless of their legal status." Verified. Sources: NPS, https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/captivity-and-independence-race-and-enslavement-in-the-trailhead-towns-along-the-missouri.htm ; St. Louis County Library, https://slcl.org/research-learn/genealogy/st-louis-genealogy/free-negroes-licensed-1835

## 12. Kalapuya history (kalapuya-history)

- **Land management, verified:** "Fire was probably the most important tool the Kalapuya used to manage the land." They burned to drive deer, clear brush under oak and hazel, "stimulate berry and root production, especially in the once extensive camas prairies," gather insects, and fertilize tobacco plots. Burning of dry prairie grass happened in late summer (and into fall). Source: Cain Allen, Oregon History Project, https://oregonhistoryproject.org/articles/historical-records/indian-burning-in-the-willamette-valley/
- **Disease before the 1840s:** malaria ("fever and ague") epidemics from about 1830 killed roughly 90 percent of Kalapuyan people within a few years; earlier epidemics, including smallpox, struck from the late 1700s. By the 1855 treaty only a few hundred Kalapuya remained. Confidence: partly verified (Oregon Encyclopedia Kalapuya Treaty entry via search snippet; Lane Community College library guide; scholarship of Robert Boyd). Sources: https://oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/kalapuya_treaty/ ; https://libraryguides.lanecc.edu/kalapuya
- **Treaty and removal:** The Willamette Valley Treaty (Treaty with the Kalapuya, etc.) was signed at Dayton January 22, 1855 and ratified March 3, 1855; it ceded the Willamette River drainage. Bands were removed to the Grand Ronde area from about February to May 1856; the Grand Ronde Reservation was set by Executive Order June 30, 1857. Confidence: verified (reservation date, Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde, https://www.grandronde.org/culture-history/treaties/); partly verified (treaty and removal dates via Oregon Encyclopedia snippet).

## 13. Irish Catholic emigrants and nativism (nativism-1840s)

- In the 1840s Irish immigrants made up nearly half of all immigrants to the U.S. Anti-Catholic and anti-immigrant feeling produced the nativist American Party ("Know-Nothings"); 1844 riots in Philadelphia left about 13 dead (LOC). Confidence: partly verified (loc.gov classroom pages via search snippet). Source: https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/immigration/irish/religious-conflict-and-discrimination/
- **Do not overstate Irish presence on the trail.** Most famine Irish settled in Eastern and Midwestern cities. The Oregon History Project counts 139 Irish immigrants holding Oregon Donation Land Claims in the 1840s and 1850s; Irish Catholics were drawn to the Catholic center at St. Paul on French Prairie. Verified. Source: https://oregonhistoryproject.org/articles/historical-records/irish-immigration/
- After the 1847 killings at the Whitman mission, Protestant missionary Henry Spalding blamed Catholic priests, and anti-Catholic feeling "flared up in Oregon." Partly verified (NPS Historical Handbook 37 via snippet, https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/hh/37/hh37m.htm).
- **I found no documented case of a wagon company voting to expel an Irish Catholic family.** The "nativist-company" and "cross-ohio-doyles" cards are fiction built on real 1840s attitudes. That is fine for a game, but a teacher note should say they are invented scenarios, not a common documented event.

## 14. Gold discovery (gold-discovery)

- James Marshall found gold at Sutter's sawmill January 24, 1848. Verified (NPS, https://www.nps.gov/cali/learn/historyculture/california-gold-rush.htm).
- President Polk confirmed the discovery in his annual message on December 5, 1848, which "triggered a mass exodus to California." Verified (Library of Congress, https://www.loc.gov/collections/california-first-person-narratives/articles-and-essays/early-california-history/discovery-of-gold/ ; text at Miller Center, https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/december-5-1848-fourth-annual-message-congress). So in 1849, gold news did not "reach" emigrants at Fort Hall; most had left Missouri because of it.

## 15. Fort Laramie (fort-laramie)

- Began as Fort William (1834), a fur trading post of William Sublette and partners, then the adobe Fort John of the American Fur Company. "The United States military bought Fort John in 1849, and the first soldiers arrived in July of that year." It served emigrants as a place "to resupply... and as a hospital." Over 25 Native nations traded there. The 1851 Horse Creek Treaty was signed about 30 miles east. Verified. Source: https://home.nps.gov/fola/what-is-fort-laramie.htm ; purchase from Bruce Husband for $4,000 in June 1849 (partly verified, Wikipedia).
- Prices at Fort Laramie in 1849 (the card's "three times" Independence prices): not verified.

## 16. Diary and letter quotes (exact text, original spelling)

All quotes below were checked against scans of the original publications on the Internet Archive, unless noted. Words in [brackets] were cut off or garbled at the line edge in the OCR and are reconstructed; check them against the page image before printing. The American Yawp Reader's modern transcription of Knight differs from the 1933 printing in small ways (for example "had drowned" for "had been drowned" and "There is not ferry" for "There is no ferry"), so do not copy from Yawp.

Copyright note: Narcissa Whitman (published 1893) and the Richey and Akin letters (published 1912) are public domain. Amelia Stewart Knight's diary was first printed in 1933 (Transactions of the 56th Annual Reunion of the Oregon Pioneer Association, "for 1928"). Works published in 1933 are public domain only if the copyright was not renewed; I could not confirm renewal status. Short classroom excerpts are low risk, but if the lesson requires strict public domain, check this.

**Departure and hope**
- Amelia Stewart Knight, April 9, 1853 (first entry): "STARTED FROM HOME about 11 o'clock and traveled 8 miles and camped in an old house; night cold and frosty." Source: https://archive.org/details/transactionsofan56oreg (p. 38). Verified.
- Knight, June 1, 1853, on the Platte, after a day of rain: "take us all together we are a poor looking set, and all this for Oregon. I am thinking while I write, "Oh, Oregon, you must be a wonderful country."" Source: same, p. 42 area. Verified (OCR has "lookinn" and "yon,," which are scan errors for "looking" and "you").

**Chimney Rock**
- Knight, June 2, 1853: "Passed Court House Rock and Chimney Rock, both situated on the lower side of the river, and have been in sight [for] several days. We have camped opposite Chimney Rock." Source: same, p. 42 to 43. Verified except the bracketed word.

**Independence Rock**
- Knight, June 15, 1853: "Came 19 miles today; passed Independence Roc[k] this afternoon, and crossed Sweetwater River on a bridge. Paid 3 dolla[rs] a wagon and swam the stock across." Source: same, p. 44 area. Verified except bracketed letters. (She reached the rock on June 15, well ahead of the Fourth of July.)

**River crossing**
- Knight, May 8, 1853, at the Elkhorn River (Nebraska): "In this way we have to cross everything a litt[le] at a time. Women and children last, and then swim the cattle and horses. There were three horses and some cattle drowned while crossing this place yesterday." Source: same, p. 40 area. Verified except bracketed letters.
- Knight, May 5, 1853, Missouri River: "One ox jumped overboard and swam across the river, and came out like a drowned rat." Verified.

**Graves, drowning, and cholera**
- Knight, May 6, 1853: "We have just passed the Mormon graveyard. There is a great number of graves on it." Verified.
- Knight, May 6, 1853: "Here we passed a train of wagons on their way back, the head man had been drowned a few days before, in a river called Elkhorn, while getting some cattle across, and his wife was lying in the wagon quite sick, and children were mourning for a father gone. With sadness and pity I passed those who perhaps a few days before had been well and happy as ourselves." Verified (the word "I" is lost at the line edge in OCR but present in the Yawp text).
- Caleb Richey, letter, "Twenty miles east of Ft. Laramie, June 20, 1852": "There is a great deal of diarrhoea and cholera on the plains. We have seen hundreds of graves, and it is said we are in advance of the greater portion of the emigration and sickness." Source: Transactions of the 40th Annual Reunion of the Oregon Pioneer Association (1912), https://archive.org/details/transactionsofan40oreg. Verified.
- James Akin, Jr., letter, about 10 miles east of Fort Laramie, June 19, 1852: "We passed from five to eight fresh graves of a day. The principal part of the sickness is the diarrhoea and cholera. Some have died with cholera in four hours after taking it. The people think the using of bad water is the cause of the sickness." Same source. Verified. (Useful for the cholera card: some emigrants already suspected bad water.)

**Bison**
- Narcissa Whitman, letter, Platte River, June 1836: "I never saw any thing like buffalo meat to satisfy hunger." and "Herds of buffalo hove in sight; one, a bull, crossed our trail and ran upon the bluffs near the rear of the camp." Source: Transactions of the 21st Annual Reunion of the Oregon Pioneer Association (1893), https://archive.org/details/transactionsof1s21oreg. Verified. (1836 is outside the game window; label it with its year.)

**Native trade**
- Knight, June 7, 1853, near Fort Laramie: "Some of the women had moccasins and beads, which they wanted [t]o trade for bread." Verified except bracketed letter.
- Knight, Aug. 17, 1853: "Bought some fresh salmon from the Indians this evening, which is quite a treat to us." Verified (Yawp text; matches OCR start of line).

**Arrival in Oregon**
- Knight, Sept. 17, 1853, and her closing note: "A few days later my eighth child was born. After this we picked up and ferried across the Columbia River, utilizing skiff, canoes and flatboat to get across, taking three days to complete. Here husband traded two yoke of oxen for a half section of land with one-half acre planted to potatoes and a small log cabin and lean-to with no windows. This is the journey's end." Source: same, p. 53 to 54. Verified (both the OCR and Yawp agree; OCR loses first letters of several lines). Note: the Knights settled north of the Columbia, in what became Clark County, Washington Territory.

## MYTHS to avoid

- **"Indian attacks were the main danger."** Disease, mainly cholera, killed by far the most emigrants. Unruh counted 362 emigrants and 426 Native people killed in clashes over 20 years, and about 90 percent of the emigrant deaths happened west of South Pass, not on the Plains.
- **"Wagon trains were often massacred."** Unruh found only four well-documented wagon-train massacres before the Civil War (verified, OCTA sheet). (The common claim that wagons were circled mainly to corral livestock is widely repeated but I did not verify it in this pass.)
- **"Pioneers traveled alone into empty wilderness."** The trail was crowded (Knight counted "three hundred or more wagons in sight" at the Elkhorn in 1853; Nebraska sources say up to 2,000 emigrants a day passed Fort Kearny at the 1850s peak, partly verified), and the land belonged to Native nations who traded, guided, ran ferries, and charged tolls.
- **"Everyone rode in the wagon."** Wagons were full of supplies and most people walked. (Widely stated; not checked against a source in this pass.)
- **"Prairie schooners" were Conestoga wagons.** Emigrants mostly used ordinary farm wagons with canvas covers, not big Conestoga freight wagons. (General knowledge; not checked in this pass.)
- **"Oregon was a free and open land."** Oregon outlawed slavery but also tried to keep out Black settlers (1844, 1849, 1857), and its land law gave free land to white settlers only, on land taken from Native nations.
- **"The Donner Party were typical."** Their disaster came from a bad cutoff and a late start. Most companies reached California or Oregon.
- **"Cholera came from the Plains."** It came from Eastern and river cities and traveled west with emigrants (St. Louis had over 4,000 cholera deaths in 1849, per the Clay paper citing Rosenberg, The Cholera Years; partly verified).
- **"1 in 10 emigrants died" stated as fact.** It is the high-end estimate. Unruh estimated closer to 4 percent. Present it as a range.
- **"Independence Rock was named for July 4 arrivals of emigrant wagons."** It was named by fur trader William Sublette's party in 1830.
- **Avoid "Manifest Destiny" as if all Americans believed it.** It was a political slogan from Democratic expansionists, and the essay's author is debated.
